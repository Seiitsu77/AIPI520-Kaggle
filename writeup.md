# Writeup: Predicting 2014–15 Subscription Purchases

## Data Pipeline and Feature Engineering

Since the labels and submission results are based on accounts, I adopted the data structure of "one row per account". Firstly, I summarize the subscription and single-ticket purchases records according to the account ID and then connect the account information with the ZIP code information. Then, I aligned the features according to the ID in the training set and the test set. The two parts of the data use the same feature-building functions to ensure the same processing method. The final feature matrix contains 47 columns, excluding the missing indicator variables additionally generated during model preprocessing.

Since we got time-series data, my model only uses the information that can be obtained when predicting to avoid data leakage. The subscription record is up to the 2013–2014 concert season, and the single-ticket purchase record covers the 2010–2011 to 2013–2014 concert season. I use the RFM framework to organize the features, that is, the time of the most recent participation, the frequency of participation and the level of monetary investment.

The subscription history first answers the most direct question: has this account formed a subscription habit, and does this habit continue? Exploratory analysis shows that among the accounts that the most recent subscription started in 2013, 82% purchased subscriptions for the target concert season; the most recent subscription started in 2012, 2011 and 2010, and the proportion dropped to 13%, 8% and 5% respectively. In the training data, there is no positive case for the last subscription to the account that occurred in 2009 or earlier.

In order to express this difference, I established binary subscription indicators for the five concert seasons from 2009 to 2013, so that the model can assign different weights to different years. Earlier records are summarized by the total number of subscription seasons, subscription span and subscription ratio within the span. I also added the number of subscriptions of the last 3, 5 and 10 concert seasons to describe the level of activity in different time frames. These windows were pre-selected by me and chosen in advance rather than tuned.

However, counting only the number of times will ignore the continuity. Both customers may have subscribed to ten seasons, but one has subscribed for ten consecutive years, and the other has subscribed intermittently. Therefore, I added the consecutive-season subscription streak up to the 2013–2014 concert season. Among 2013–2014 subscribers, this feature alone achieved an AUROC of 0.77 for predicting purchases in the following season. I also added the average number of seats, subscription tier, price level and share of Full packages to express the mode of participation and the degree of investment.

For accounts without subscription history, I set the relevant count to zero and add a historical indicator. I filled in the "years since the last subscription" as 99 as a placeholder value without history. This can distinguish between accounts that have not been subscribed to and past subscribers, but it will also introduce an extreme value to logistic regression. Therefore, I specially tested other recency encodings in the second round of experiments.

In addition to the subscription history, the answer to the single-ticket purchase record is: which customers who have not subscribed are still participating in the performance? Among the accounts that did not purchase 2013–2014 subscriptions, 38% of the positive cases that finally purchased the next season's subscription bought single tickets, while only 10% of the negative cases. Therefore, I added the number of tickets purchased, the total number of seats, the number of ticketed seasons, a 2013-2014 ticket indicator and the average numeric price level. The price field contains words such as "Adult", "Youth", "GA", etc., and I will treat the values that cannot be converted into numbers as missing.

The donation reflects the participation outside of buying tickets. In 2013, 66% of accounts with donations purchased target subscriptions, and the proportion of accounts without donations was 2.4%. I used the number of donations, the 2013 and lifetime donation amounts, the average single amount and the year of the first donation. The median of cumulative donations is zero, but the maximum value reaches 1.25 million. Therefore, I have made log transformations of skewed variables such as amounts to narrow the gap between extreme values and ordinary values.

However, the strong relationship of donations also needs to be understood in combination with time. The first donation date includes records from April to September 2014, and some donations may occur at the same time as the subscription purchase. The final model retains these variables, so I can't interpret them all as predictive signals before buying. The lifetime donation field also needs to clarify its as-of date to confirm whether it is suitable for actual forecasting.

Finally, geographical information is used to express the convenience of travel and the regional background. I give priority to the billing ZIP code, and when it is missing, I use the shipping ZIP code, and then match the estimated population, the average wages per tax return, and the latitude and longitude of each ZIP code. I calculated the distance and the closest distance to the location of the four approximate venues. These coordinates represent the area where the venue is located, not the exact address; regional salary indicators are only proxy variables of the economic background, and do not represent the customer's own income.

In the processing of missing values, I distinguish between "no behavior record" and "unknown information". Logistic regression uses the median to fill in the remaining missing values, generates additional missing-value indicator columns, and then standardizes them. Filling and standardization fit internally in each cross-validation training fold.

## Feature Selection and Model Development

My screening principle is simple: if the additional details do not show clear benefits, consider deleting them; if you want to add a new complex structure, you need stronger validation evidence. I ablate by feature group, instead of selecting variables one by one, so that I can not only judge around business assumptions but also reduce the number of repeated selections based on cross-validation results.

The first round of ablation supports the retention of single tickets, geography and account information. After removing the single-ticket features, the AUROC of logistic regression decreased by 0.0037; the geographic features decreased by 0.0041; and the account marking decreased by 0.0018. These results show that although the subscription history is important, it does not contain all the useful information.

In contrast, after removing the subscription details of the previous season, AUROC increased by 0.0004; after removing the primary-venue indicators, it increased by 0.0003. I don't regard these small changes as significant improvements but use them as the basis for simplifying features. Last season's subscription details mainly focused on 345 accounts, and may be duplicated with historical average seats, tiers and other information. The venue indicators also further dispersed the sample. I also deleted the total number of subscription records because it was duplicated with the number of subscription seasons in the existing data. These ablations belong to the exploration results of the feature selection stage, and it cannot be determined that a small difference is statistically significant.

I also deliberately didn't add some finer features. For example, the single-ticket table has only 2,808 rows, covering about 10% of the accounts, so composers or repertoire preferences may be too sparse. I didn't use city target encoding, and I didn't use account ID as a predictive feature to reduce the dependence on small sample categories and data sets.

In terms of models, I compared single-feature baselines, logistic regression, Random Forest, Extra Trees and LightGBM. Logistic regression can directly use the behavioral features that have been constructed and limit the size of the coefficient by regularizing. Tree models can detect nonlinear relationships and interactions, but it is also easier to fit accidental patterns in a small number of positive examples.

Final hyperparameter tuning uses a small, preset search grid. Logistic regression compares the configuration of C as 0.01, 0.03, 0.1 and 0.3, and finally selects C = 0.03 on all training data. Extra Trees tunes the minimum number of samples per leaf and the fraction of features sampled at each split, and LightGBM adjusts the number of trees, the number of leaf nodes and the minimum number of samples per child node. I also tried an equal-weight rank-average ensemble of those three models and converted the predictions of each model into a ranking average without learning ensemble weights.

## Validation and Results

The ultimate goal of this project is to let real buyers get higher prediction rankings, rather than optimizing the accuracy rate under a fixed threshold. This is because, with only 4.8% positives, predicting no one buys would already give 95% accuracy. Since there are few positive cases, I use stratified cross-validation to keep the proportion of positive cases close to each fold. The preliminary comparison uses 5 fold cross-validation and repeats it 5 times.

Finally, the comparison uses nested cross-validation: the outer loop is 5 fold and repeated 3 times; the hyperparameters are selected with 5 fold cross-validation inside each outer training set and then evaluated on the corresponding outer validation fold. At the same time, I record the training AUROC to observe whether the more complex models mainly improve the performance of the training set.

| Model Candidate       | Mean Outer AUROC | Fold Standard Deviation |
|-----------------------|------------------|-------------------------|
| Logistic Regression   | 0.9786           | 0.0076                  |
| Extra Trees           | 0.977            | 0.0078                  |
| LightGBM              | 0.9754           | 0.0093                  |
| Rank-average ensemble | 0.978            | 0.0079                  |

The average validation performance of logistic regression is the highest, and the gap between training and validation is also the smallest. This result is consistent with the exploratory analysis: the purchase rate decreases significantly with the subscription time, and the consecutive-season streak can distinguish recent subscribers. After expressing these relationships through annual indicators, frequency and streak features, logistic regression has been able to use a large amount of information. The higher training performance of the tree model has not been converted into higher validation performance, indicating that the extra flexibility has not brought stable gains in this group of experiments. However, the gap between the models is relatively small, and I did not interpret this result as logistic regression is significantly better than other models.

The second round of experiments further tested this judgment. I tried a new recency encoding, the interaction with the subscription status of the previous season, the combination of the two, a spline transformation, and the establishment of models for the previous season's subscribers and other accounts respectively. The criteria adopted are the mean paired AUROC improvement on the same CV split is at least 0.002, the new version is better on least 70% of the folds, and it passes again on CV splits from a second random seed.

All the plans did not meet the standard. Among them, the combination of recency and interaction terms increased by 0.0013 and 0.0010 on the two sets of CV splits, but it was still not enough to support the increase in complexity. The second random seed check is the sensitivity of the results to the CV splits, not an independent test set.

I submitted two predictions: one is from logistic regression, and another one is from the rank-average ensemble. Their public scores are 0.9737 and 0.97198 respectively. I eventually chose logistic regression because of the higher public score, which is also consistent with local CV. I know this gap itself is not enough to prove that the model has a clear advantage. But logistic regression is simpler and easier to interpret, which is also a reason to support the final choice.
