# Writeup: Predicting 2014–15 Subscription Purchases

## Data Pipeline and Feature Engineering

Since the labels and submission results are based on accounts, I build a row of data for each account: summarize subscription records and single ticket records by account ID, connect the account table and the zip code table, and use the same feature construction function for the training set and the test set. The final feature matrix has 47 features, in addition to the missing value indicator column added during preprocessing.

In order to avoid data leakage, I only use the records before the 2014–15 season, with only one exception, which will be discussed below (the timing of the donation). I use the RFM framework to organize the features: recency, frequency and monetary value of participation.

The subscription history first answers the most direct question: has this account formed a subscription habit, and does this habit continue? Exploratory analysis shows that 82% of the accounts with the most recent subscription in the 2013–14 season purchased the subscription for the 2014–15 season. The last subscription was in the 2012–13, 2011–12 or 2010–11 seasons, and the percentage dropped to 13%, 8% and 5% respectively. Among training accounts whose last subscription was in the 2009–10 season or earlier, there was no purchase.

In order to express this difference, I built a binary indicator variable for each season from 2009–10 to 2013–14, so that the model can give a separate weight to each year. The earlier history can be summarized in three quantities: the number of seasons subscribed, the span from the first season to the last season, and the proportion of subscription seasons within the span. I also counted the number of subscriptions in the last 3, 5 and 10 seasons. These windows are pre-selected and have not been tuned.

Only the number of times will ignore the continuity: of two customers who have each subscribed to ten seasons, one may have subscribed for ten consecutive years, and the other may have subscribed intermittently. Therefore, I added the number of consecutive subscription seasons (streak) as of the 2013–14 season. Among the subscribers in the 2013–14 season, the AUROC of this one feature alone reached 0.77. I also added the average number of seats, subscription tier, price level and share of Full packages to describe the level of investment of each customer.

For accounts without subscription history, I set the count to 0, added an indicator variable of "whether there is a subscription history", and filled in the "years since the last subscription" as a placeholder value of 99. This can distinguish between accounts that have never subscribed and past subscribers, but it will also bring an extreme value to logistic regression, so I tested other recency encodings in the second round of experiments.

The single ticket record answers another question: who is still coming to the concert without subscription? Among the accounts without subscriptions for the 2013–14 season, 38% of those who eventually bought a subscription had bought single tickets, compared with only 10% of those who did not. Therefore, I added the number of ticket purchases, the total number of seats, the number of ticketing seasons, the 2013–14 season ticketing indicator variable and the average numeric price level.

Donations reflect participation other than buying tickets. Among the accounts with donations in 2013, 66% purchased subscriptions for the 2014–15 season, and only 2.4% of accounts without donations. I used the number of donations, the amount of donations in 2013 and the cumulative amount of donations, the average single amount, and the year of the first donation. The amount of donation is highly skewed: the median in the training set is 0, and the maximum value is 1.25 million. So I made a log transformation of the amount and other skewed variables.

However, the timing of the donation needs to be treated with caution. Some first donations date between April and September 2014, and 13 of the 14 such accounts in the training set purchased subscriptions. Therefore, part of the donation signal may come from the purchasing behavior itself, rather than predicting the purchase. In order to set an upper limit for this risk, I removed all the donation features in an ablation experiment, and AUROC only decreased by 0.0036, indicating that the model does not mainly rely on them. I kept these features, but I didn't take them all as signals available before the purchase.

Finally, geographical information describes the convenience of travel and the regional background. I use the billing zip code, and when it is missing, I use the shipping zip code, add the estimated population of the region, the average wages per tax return, and the distance to the four approximate venues and the distance to the nearest venue. The salary at the zip code level is only a proxy variable of the regional economic background and does not represent the customer's own income.

In the processing of missing values, I distinguish between "no record" (the count is set to 0) and "unknown" (retained as missing). For logistic regression, the remaining missing values are filled with the median and missing-value indicator columns are added, and then standardized. The imputer and scaler are only fit on the training part of each CV fold.

## Feature Selection and Model Development

I ablate by feature group, instead of filtering individual variables. In this way, each test corresponds to a business hypothesis, and the number of choices made according to the CV results is much less, thus reducing the risk of overfitting the CV results themselves.

The first round of ablation (logistic regression, C = 0.1, 5-fold CV repeated 5 times) supports the retention of three feature groups (single ticket, geography and account): after removing them respectively, AUROC decreased by 0.0037, 0.0041 and 0.0018. The account group contains two indicator variables: whether the account has a relationship record and whether it has a shipping zip code.

In contrast, after removing the subscription details of the previous season, AUROC rose by 0.0004; after removing the main venue indicator variable, it rose by 0.0003. The subscription details of the 2013–14 season are non-zero only for the 345 training accounts that subscribed in that season, and overlap with historical average seats, levels and other information. I also deleted the total number of subscription records, because it is exactly the same as the number of subscription seasons in this data. These differences are very small. I don't think they are statistically significant. I just use them to streamline the feature set. Since the feature selection of this step is done before the nested CV and on the entire training set, the following nested CV score may be slightly optimistic.

I deliberately didn't add some more detailed features. For example, the single-ticket table has only 2,808 rows, covering only about 10% of the accounts, so features such as composers or repertoire preferences may be too sparse. I didn't use the city target encoding, because many cities only have a few accounts. I didn't use the account ID either: it is the primary key of the database, and the prefix reflects how the record is created, not the customer's behavior. The positive rate of one of the prefix groups is as high as 69%, suggesting that there is a leak. And adding it does not bring any CV improvement (+0.0000).

In terms of models, I compared single-feature baselines, logistic regression, Extra Trees and LightGBM. Regularized logistic regression directly uses the constructed features. Tree models can detect nonlinear effects and interactions, but in the case of only 332 positive examples, it is also easy to fit accidental patterns. Since the seasonal indicator variable has depicted the main nonlinearity, I expect logistic regression to be competitive and use the tree model to test this expectation. In the first round of comparison (5-fold CV repeated 5 times), the number of consecutive subscription seasons alone reached 0.922, and the full feature set about 0.978. In addition, I deliberately trained a very deep, almost unregularized LightGBM, which shows what the overfitting looks like here: training AUROC 0.9998, but CV AUROC is only 0.9575.

Final tuning uses a small preset grid. The C of logistic regression takes {0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3}, and selects C = 0.03 on all training data.

## Validation and Results

The ultimate goal of this project is to make real buyers rank higher in the forecast, rather than optimizing the accuracy rate under a fixed threshold. The accuracy rate here is also misleading: positive cases are only 4.8%, and the accuracy rate is already 95% if everyone is predicted as "not to buy". Due to the scarcity of positive cases, I use stratified 5-fold cross-validation (repeated 5 times in the first round of comparison), so that the proportion of buyers in each fold is similar. Before relying on CV, I first confirmed that the accounts of the training set and the test set come from the same distribution: a classifier trained to distinguish between the two has an AUROC of only 0.49.

The final comparison uses nested cross-validation: the outer loop is 5-fold and repeated 3 times; the hyperparameters are selected with 5-fold cross-validation within each outer training set, and then evaluated on the corresponding outer validation fold. I also recorded the training AUROC to observe whether the more complex models mainly improve the fit of the training data.

| Model Candidate | Mean Outer AUROC | Fold Standard Deviation | Train AUROC | Gap |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| Logistic Regression | 0.9786 | 0.0076 | 0.9820 | 0.0035 |
| Extra Trees | 0.9770 | 0.0078 | 0.9921 | 0.0151 |
| LightGBM | 0.9754 | 0.0093 | 0.9865 | 0.0111 |

The average validation performance of logistic regression is the highest, and the gap between training and validation is also the smallest. This is consistent with the exploratory analysis: season indicator variables, counts and the number of consecutive subscription seasons have depicted the law of "the longer the last subscription, the lower the purchase rate". After that, the linear model can take advantage of most of the signals. The higher training AUROC of tree models has not been converted into validation performance, indicating that their additional flexibility has not brought about a stable improvement. However, the gap between the models (0.0016–0.0032) is much smaller than the standard deviation (about 0.008), so I don't think logistic regression is significantly better than other models.

The second round of experiments further tested this judgment. I tried five variants: replace the placeholder value 99 with a bounded recency encoding; interaction terms of key features and subscription status of 2013–14 season; the combination of the above two; spline transformation; model separately for subscribers and others in the 2013–14 season.

Before running these experiments, I set the acceptance criteria: on the same CV splits, the mean paired AUROC improvement is at least 0.002; the new version is better on at least 70% of the folds; on CV splits generated with a second random seed, these two are still valid.

None of the variants meets this standard. The closest thing is the recency encoding plus interaction terms: it is better on 84% and 80% of the folds respectively, but the average increase (0.0013 and 0.0010 on the two sets of splits respectively) is lower than the threshold of 0.002, which I don't think is enough to support the additional complexity.

My final model is the logistic regression of C = 0.03: its nested CV AUROC is the highest, the gap between training and validation is the smallest, and it is also the simplest and easiest model to explain. Its public leaderboard score of 0.97370 is slightly lower than the CV estimate, but within the expected noise range. The public leaderboard only uses about half of the test set; I repeatedly randomly selected subsets of the same size from the out-of-fold prediction to calculate, and the standard deviation of AUROC of the same model is about 0.008. A rank-average ensemble of the three models was not better (nested CV 0.9780, public leaderboard 0.97198), so I kept the simpler model.
